Equations of State

Start here

An EOS answers questions such as “what pressure does this amount of fluid exert in this volume?” and “how far is this fluid from ideal-gas behavior?”. These are single homogeneous-phase calculations; evaluating an EOS at an overall mixture composition does not automatically split it into equilibrium phases. See thermodynamic fundamentals for the broader workflow.

For practical use: read the units below, run the complete example, then look up the property you need in the table of contents. The detailed equations are an optional reference, not a prerequisite for calling the routines.

Units and amount basis

The default unit system uses mol, L, K, and bar, with . Do not pass Pa or m³ without converting them.

  • The Fortran array n(:) contains component amounts, , in mol; the scalar total is .
  • Mole fractions are . They sum to one and specify composition, not the amount of material. Passing n=z selects a one-mole basis.
  • V is total volume in L; molar volume is in L/mol. If you multiply every amount by ten, also multiply V by ten to keep the same density and pressure.
  • Ar, Hr, Gr, and Ur are total energies in bar L, not molar energies; divide by sum(n) for bar L/mol. One bar L is 100 J. Entropy and heat capacities are in bar L/K. Derivative units follow from division by the differentiated variable, e.g. dPdV in bar/L.
  • lnPhi and lngamma are dimensionless vectors, one entry per component. Composition-derivative matrices have entry (i,j) equal to the derivative of output i with respect to amount n(j), not mole fraction z(j).

Choose a model and parameters appropriate to your fluid and range; a successful calculation is not evidence that the model is accurate there. See cubic models, GERG-2008, and PC-SAFT for model-specific inputs and limitations.

Why Helmholtz energy?

yaeos is a library based on Equation-of-State calculations. An Equation of State (EOS) is a mathematical model that describes the relationship between thermodynamic properties of a system, typically pressure (P), volume (V), and temperature (T). These equations are fundamental in physics, chemistry, and engineering, especially in thermodynamics and fluid mechanics.

EOS models are used to predict phase behavior, thermodynamic properties, and equilibrium conditions of gases, liquids, solids, and mixtures. They are widely applied in areas such as chemical engineering, petroleum engineering, and material science. From the ideal gas law, to cubic equations of state and SAFT, to more complex models, EOS are essential tools for the study and design of processes.

The classic way of defining an EOS is though a mathematical equation that express the pressure as a function of the volume and temperature. For example, the Van der Waals EOS is defined as:

Where is the pressure, is the molar volume, is the temperature, is the gas constant, and and are parameters that depend on the substance. For mixtures, the and parameters are calculated from the pure components parameters using a mixing rule.

The modern way of defining an EOS is through a mathematical model that express the residual Helmholtz free energy as a function of the mole number, volume and temperature. This approach is more general and allows for a more flexible definition of the EOS in the context of implementing it in code. All residual thermodynamic properties can be calculated from the residual Helmholtz free energy and its derivatives. Total caloric properties also require an ideal-gas contribution and a consistent reference state. yaeos is based on this approach.

This section of the documentation will guide you through the thermodynamic properties that can be calculated with yaeos using Helmholtz free energy models. Additionally, this page serves as a summary of how all these properties can be derived from the Helmholtz free energy and its derivatives.

First, you might be used to the classic way of defining an EOS. This is normal since it is the most common way of explaining them in undergraduate thermodynamics courses. A complete first calculation

This standalone program constructs a Peng–Robinson model and evaluates a vapor root. The critical constants below are illustrative inputs, not a validated parameter set for named substances.

program main
   use yaeos, only: pr, R, ArModel, PengRobinson76
   use, intrinsic :: ieee_arithmetic, only: ieee_is_finite
   implicit none

   ! We will instantiate a Peng-Robinson EOS model with two components

   ! Set the variable `model` as a generic `ArModel`
   class(ArModel), allocatable :: model

   real(pr) :: tc(2)
   real(pr) :: pc(2)
   real(pr) :: w(2)
   real(pr) :: n(2)
   real(pr) :: T
   real(pr) :: P
   real(pr) :: V
   real(pr) :: Pcheck
   real(pr) :: Z
   real(pr) :: lnPhi(2)
   real(pr) :: Hr

   tc = [190.0_pr, 310.0_pr] ! Critical temperatures [K]
   pc = [14.0_pr, 30.0_pr]   ! Critical pressures [bar]
   w = [0.001_pr, 0.03_pr]   ! Acentric factors [-]

   ! Now we set our model as the PengRobinson76 equation of state.
   model = PengRobinson76(tc, pc, w)

   n = [0.3_pr, 0.7_pr] ! One mole, composition z = [0.3, 0.7]
   T = 300.0_pr
   P = 1.0_pr
   call model%volume(n, P, T, V=V, root_type="vapor")
   if (.not. ieee_is_finite(V)) error stop "Nonfinite volume"
   if (V <= 0.0_pr) error stop "Volume solve failed"
   call model%pressure(n, V, T, P=Pcheck)
   if (.not. ieee_is_finite(Pcheck)) error stop "Nonfinite pressure"
   if (abs(Pcheck - P) > 1.0e-6_pr) error stop "Pressure mismatch"
   call model%lnphi_vt(n, V, T, lnPhi=lnPhi)
   call model%enthalpy_residual_vt(n, V, T, Hr=Hr)
   Z = Pcheck*V/(sum(n)*R*T)
   print *, "Total volume [L]: ", V
   print *, "Molar volume [L/mol]: ", V/sum(n)
   print *, "Compressibility factor [-]: ", Z
   print *, "ln(phi_i) [-]: ", lnPhi
   print *, "Residual molar enthalpy [J/mol]: ", 100.0_pr*Hr/sum(n)

end program main

Here Z=1 is ideal-gas behavior; deviations show how the pressure–volume relation differs from an ideal gas. lnPhi=0 means a unit fugacity coefficient, not zero fugacity. A negative Hr means an enthalpy below the ideal-gas value at the same temperature and composition; it is not an absolute enthalpy.

All subsequent code blocks are snippets: insert them after model construction and before end program main, not as separate programs. Their block constructs give local variables their own scope. Optional derivative outputs may be omitted when only the property itself is needed.

Volume:

At fixed amounts, pressure, and temperature, an EOS may have more than one volume root. A conventional cubic EOS has one or three real algebraic roots (counting multiplicity); not every root is physically admissible. Three distinct physical phases are not implied by three roots. Other model families need not follow this root count. yaeos uses numerical volume solvers; this is an implementation choice, not a consequence of nonuniqueness alone.

root_type="liquid" seeks the dense root and "vapor" the dilute root. They may return the same volume when only one root exists. "stable" selects the lower-Gibbs-energy candidate at the specified composition; it does not test stability against separation into phases of different compositions. For equilibrium phase amounts and compositions, use the phase-equilibrium routines.

Check that a returned volume is finite and positive, and re-evaluate pressure to check the solve, as in the first example. Root solvers can fail, especially near limiting states. A mechanically stable homogeneous branch normally has ; this is necessary, not sufficient, for full mixture stability.

To learn more about volume initialization, please refer to the book “Thermodynamic Models: Fundamentals and Computational Aspects” by Michael L. Michelsen and Jørgen M. Mollerup. Volume derivatives can be calculated in terms of pressure derivatives, this will appear later on this documentation.

volume: block
    real(pr) :: n(2)
    real(pr) :: T
    real(pr) :: P
    real(pr) :: V

    n = [0.3_pr, 0.7_pr] ! Component amounts [mol]; total 1 mol, z = [0.3, 0.7]
    T = 300.0_pr         ! Set temperature to 300 K
    P = 1.0_pr           ! Set pressure to 1 bar

    ! Calculate different volume roots
    call model%volume(n, P, T, V=V, root_type="liquid")
    print *, "Liquid volume: ", V

    call model%volume(n, P, T, V=V, root_type="vapor")
    print *, "Vapor volume: ", V

    call model%volume(n, P, T, V=V, root_type="stable")
    print *, "Stable volume root: ", V

end block volume

Excess properties (n, P, T)

Excess properties measure departure from an ideal solution, not from an ideal gas. They are not generally equal to the full change on mixing: an ideal solution already has entropy and Gibbs energy of mixing. At the same and composition,

For , the ideal-solution property is the sum of pure-component properties:

Where is the molar property of the pure component at the same pressure and temperature as the mixture.

For Gibbs and Helmholtz energies, add to this pure sum; for entropy, add . Those ideal-mixing terms must be included before interpreting , , or . For example, positive means expansion relative to the pure-volume sum, and negative means an enthalpy below the pure-enthalpy sum.

The current routines use root_type for the mixture but always use "stable" roots for the pure-component references. This matters if you want a liquid-standard-state activity coefficient while a pure component’s stable state at the specified is vapor. These routines do not automatically construct hypothetical pure-liquid reference states. Derivatives assume a smooth branch and are not derivatives across a reference-root switch.
