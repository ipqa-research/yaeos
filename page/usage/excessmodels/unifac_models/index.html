UNIFAC Models

Introduction

UNIFAC predicts liquid nonideality from a molecule’s functional groups rather than a separate fitted interaction for every pair of whole molecules. This is useful for screening solvents or estimating activity coefficients when binary mixture data are scarce. You supply a chemical decomposition; the constructor combines it with a built-in table of group sizes, areas, and interactions.

It is not a molecular simulator and does not use a chemical name or atom list directly. Molecules with the same subgroup counts are indistinguishable to a given UNIFAC variant, even if their detailed structures differ. A validated NRTL or UNIQUAC fit may be preferable for a well-characterized binary system; UNIFAC trades that specificity for transfer of group parameters between compounds.

PSRK UNIFAC setup_psrk(molecules) Group model/parameter set developed for PSRK EOS use; not itself a full EOS

The shared Fortran type does not make these models interchangeable. Select the matching group table, interaction set, and temperature function together. Changing only d or copying group IDs from another variant is not a valid model conversion. Published tables below are useful references; the parameter data bundled with your installed version determine what is actually available.

Each UNIFAC model is differentiated by the list of available functional groups, the interaction parameters between functional groups, the value of the exponent in the combinatorial term, and the function of temperature used in the residual term. Check the section of each model for more specific details of each model. Let’s begin:

UNIFAC (UNIQUAC Functional-group Activity Coefficients) is an Excess Gibbs free energy model used to estimate activity coefficients in non-ideal mixtures. It is particularly useful for predicting the phase behavior of chemical mixtures, including liquid-liquid equilibrium (LLE) and vapor-liquid equilibrium (VLE). In this model the Excess Gibbs free energy is calculated from the contribution of a combinatorial term and a residual term.

Being:

  • Combinatorial: Accounts for the size and shape of the molecules.

  • Residual: Accounts for the energy interactions between different functional groups.

“Residual” here names a contribution to excess Gibbs energy relative to an ideal liquid, not a residual departure from an ideal gas as in an EOS. Differentiating the sum gives . The dimensionless activity is , and converting to liquid fugacity requires a pure-liquid reference fugacity. See Gibbs Excess Models for units, reference states, and why a liquid activity model alone is not a vapor-liquid flash model.

Each substance of a mixture modeled with UNIFAC must be represented by a list a functional groups and other list with the ocurrence of each functional group on the substance.

The physical picture

Two ideas explain almost everything about UNIFAC results.

Size and shape. A big molecule dilutes itself more than a small one, and a compact molecule presents a different surface than a floppy one. The combinatorial term handles this with the two numbers (relative volume) and (relative surface area), which are sums of subgroup contributions. Ethanol and 1-propanol differ in a single CH2 group, so their and differ by that group’s contributions, and their residual behavior differs only through the groups they contain.

Energy of unlike contacts. Whether an OH group finds water or ethanol molecules around it changes its chemical potential. The residual term handles this with group-group interactions, and it is where the “residual” name comes from. Because interactions are stored between groups rather than molecules, information transfers: knowing that water likes ethanol’s OH group also tells you something about water with any other alcohol.

The practical consequence is that UNIFAC can rank or screen solvents without any mixture data, but its accuracy is inherited from the fitted group interactions. A group pair that was never fitted contributes nothing useful, and no error message will tell you that.

Preparing inputs safely

  1. Choose the variant before identifying fragments. A subgroup identifies a specific fragment (for example CH3); a main group collects subgroups that share interaction parameters. Pass subgroup IDs, not main-group IDs.
  2. For each component, set molecules(i)%groups_ids and molecules(i)%number_of_groups to integer arrays of equal length. Counts are positive occurrences per molecule, not amounts in the mixture. List each subgroup once with its total count and do not double-count atoms in compound fragments (for example methanol has a dedicated CH3OH group).
  3. Check coverage of all required directional main-group interactions, not only whether each subgroup exists. A missing interaction is not evidence of an ideal or zero-energy contact. The constructors’ dimensional checks cannot establish scientific coverage or suitability for your mixture.
  4. Construct the model once, then vary n(:) (mol) and T (K). Component order in n follows molecules. Require nonnegative amounts and a positive total. Do not substitute mass fractions or Celsius temperatures.

Subgroup numbers are variant-specific

The same integer does not mean the same fragment in different variants. In the original UNIFAC set, subgroup 118 is the sulfone fragment (CH2)2SU; in the PSRK set, subgroup 118 is methane CH4. Passing a number from one variant’s table into another variant’s constructor therefore builds a model for a different chemistry, and the program will not complain: the lookup falls back to whatever the parameter table happens to hold, and the results are silently wrong.

Two practical consequences:

  • Always take subgroup numbers from the table of the variant you are constructing. Each variant page in this section has its own table.
  • If a result looks absurd (a coefficient of order 10^-700, or a coefficient that jumps by orders of magnitude between two similar mixtures), suspect a misidentified or out-of-table subgroup before suspecting the physics.

Molecular size and area parameters are sums of subgroup contributions, and , where is the count of subgroup k in component i. R_k and Q_k are dimensionless relative volumes and areas, not physical SI measurements.

Examples

On each specific UNIFAC model section we will give more examples of each specific model. Here we leave a simple example for the classic Liquid-Vapor UNIFAC model.

Calculating activity coefficients

We can instantiate a UNIFAC model with a mixture ethanol-water and evaluate the logarithm of activity coefficients of the model for a 0.5 mole fraction of each, and a temperature of 298.0 K.

program unifac_activity
use yaeos, only: pr, Groups, UNIFAC, setup_unifac
implicit none

! Variables declarations
type(UNIFAC) :: model
type(Groups) :: molecules(2)
real(pr) :: ln_gammas(2)
real(pr) :: n(2)
real(pr) :: T

! Variables instances
! Ethanol definition [CH3, CH2, OH]
molecules(1)%groups_ids = [1, 2, 14] ! Subgroups ids
molecules(1)%number_of_groups = [1, 1, 1] ! Subgroups occurrences

! Water definition [H2O]
molecules(2)%groups_ids = [16]
molecules(2)%number_of_groups = [1]

! Model setup
model = setup_unifac(molecules)

! Calculate ln_gammas
n = [0.5_pr, 0.5_pr]
T = 298.0_pr
call model%ln_activity_coefficient(n, T, ln_gammas)

print *, "ln(gamma): ethanol, water = ", ln_gammas
print *, "gamma = ", exp(ln_gammas)
print *, "activity = ", n/sum(n)*exp(ln_gammas)
end program unifac_activity

This is a complete program. Each output array is ordered ethanol, then water. ln_gammas are natural logarithms, exp(ln_gammas) are coefficients, and the final line multiplies by the mole fractions to obtain activities. Positive logarithms mean coefficients greater than 1; neither coefficients nor activities should be interpreted as percentages.

Worked example: the same mixture through two variants

Because the three variants share the same Fortran type, you can compare them with one extra program. This is a good way to see what the “variant” choice actually changes.

program unifac_variants
use yaeos, only: pr, Groups, UNIFAC
use yaeos, only: setup_unifac, setup_dortmund, setup_psrk
implicit none

type(UNIFAC) :: model_lv
type(UNIFAC) :: model_do
type(UNIFAC) :: model_ps
type(Groups) :: molecules(2)
real(pr) :: ln_lv(2)
real(pr) :: ln_do(2)
real(pr) :: ln_ps(2)
real(pr) :: n(2)
real(pr) :: T

molecules(1)%groups_ids = [1, 2, 14]
molecules(1)%number_of_groups = [1, 1, 1]
molecules(2)%groups_ids = [16]
molecules(2)%number_of_groups = [1]

n = [0.5_pr, 0.5_pr]
T = 298.0_pr

model_lv = setup_unifac(molecules)
model_do = setup_dortmund(molecules)
model_ps = setup_psrk(molecules)

call model_lv%ln_activity_coefficient(n, T, ln_lv)
call model_do%ln_activity_coefficient(n, T, ln_do)
call model_ps%ln_activity_coefficient(n, T, ln_ps)

print *, "original UNIFAC ln(gamma) = ", ln_lv
print *, "Dortmund     ln(gamma) = ", ln_do
print *, "PSRK         ln(gamma) = ", ln_ps
end program unifac_variants

The Dortmund answer differs from the other two. That is expected: each variant has its own fitted group interactions and its own geometry, so in general the numbers are independent estimates rather than one number computed several ways. For many ordinary mixtures the PSRK parameter set reuses the same size parameters and a values as the original model for the common main groups; for this ethanol-water example the original and PSRK results coincide, while the Dortmund set, with its modified size term and fitted interactions, differs. A visible difference is a reminder that these are predictions from different data; it is not a bug.

Worked example: a liquid-liquid split

Activity models are usually used through the phase-equilibrium routines rather than directly. The program below uses UNIFAC to find a two-liquid split of a ternary feed, which is the classic use case for a group-contribution model: screening which components separate without binary-specific fits.

program unifac_lle
use yaeos, only: pr, Groups, UNIFAC, setup_unifac
use yaeos, only: EquilibriumState, flash, min_tpd
implicit none

integer, parameter :: nc = 3

type(UNIFAC) :: model
type(Groups) :: molecules(nc)
type(EquilibriumState) :: result
real(pr) :: n(nc)
real(pr) :: w(nc)
real(pr) :: T
real(pr) :: P
real(pr) :: mintpd
integer :: iters

molecules(1)%groups_ids = [1, 42]
molecules(1)%number_of_groups = [1, 1]

molecules(2)%groups_ids = [1, 2]
molecules(2)%number_of_groups = [2, 4]

molecules(3)%groups_ids = [16]
molecules(3)%number_of_groups = [1]

model = setup_unifac(molecules)

n = [0.2_pr, 0.7_pr, 0.1_pr]
T = 250.0_pr
P = 1.0_pr

call min_tpd(model, n, P=P, T=T, mintpd=mintpd, w=w)
print *, "mintpd = ", mintpd

if (mintpd < 0.0_pr) then
   result = flash(model, n, T, k0=w/n, iters=iters)
   print *, "kind = ", result%kind
   print *, "x = ", result%x
   print *, "y = ", result%y
   print *, "beta = ", result%beta
else
   print *, "Feed is stable in this search."
end if
end program unifac_lle

min_tpd looks for an instability of the feed; a negative value means the mixture wants to split. flash then solves the isofugacity and mass-balance equations at the given temperature, starting from the trial composition. For an activity model it requires the initial K-factors k0 and cannot accept a pressure or volume specification. Check result%kind before using x, y, and beta; the pressure and phase volumes are not set for a GeModel result.

The flash page describes this workflow in more detail, including how to initialize from a previous nearby calculation when building tables of phase equilibria.

Checks, limitations, and next steps

A few quick checks catch most mistakes before they become wrong results.

Check the amount scaling. Multiply every amount by a common factor and confirm that the activity coefficients do not change, while the extensive Ge, He, and Se scale with that factor. A model that fails this test has a normalization problem.

Check the pure-component limit. Approach a pure composition and confirm that the coefficient of the present component tends to 1. This does not require a trace solute’s coefficient to approach 1: a solute at infinite dilution has its own limiting coefficient. The pure-group reference subtraction in the residual term enforces this standard-state convention, and it matters even for molecules made of several groups.

Check the decomposition before the physics. When results look wrong, look at the group assignment first. A wrong OH subgroup, an omitted ring-specific fragment, or a double-counted compound fragment changes both the geometric and the energetic contribution at once, so there is no way to detect it from the output alone.

Know the limits. Prediction quality depends on the fitted coverage of the groups involved and on the temperature range of that fit. More temperature coefficients do not guarantee reliable extrapolation. The standard variants are not general electrolyte or speciation models, and neither liquid density nor vapor properties follow from an isolated UNIFAC object.

Continue with the variant’s subgroup tables and examples, then use the shared excess-property methods for thermal properties and derivatives. Compare with experimental data before drawing process-design or phase-split conclusions. A trial liquid’s coefficients alone do not establish its stability; see phase equilibrium.
