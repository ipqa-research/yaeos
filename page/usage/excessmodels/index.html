Gibbs Excess Models

What problem does an excess-Gibbs model solve?

Mixing water with an alcohol does not simply dilute two otherwise unchanged liquids: each molecule encounters new neighbors. An excess-Gibbs model describes how these unlike contacts change the chemical potential (the driving force for transfer or separation) relative to an ideal liquid solution. In yaeos, these models extend GeModel and calculate activity coefficients and excess thermal properties from temperature and amounts. They do not calculate liquid density or vapor properties on their own.

An excess property compares a real mixture with an ideal solution at the same temperature, pressure, and composition:

Unlike excess enthalpy, excess Gibbs energy and excess entropy are not the total changes on mixing. An ideal solution already has an entropy of mixing:

A residual property in an equation of state instead measures departure from an ideal gas reference. It is not interchangeable with an excess property. Also, the word “residual” inside UNIQUAC/UNIFAC names just their energetic contribution to the excess Gibbs energy, not an ideal-gas departure. The thermodynamics essentials page develops this distinction in more depth, including why an excess quantity cannot be added to a residual one.

Activity, activity coefficient, and fugacity

With a pure-liquid standard state, the dimensionless activity is , and . The activity coefficient corrects ideal dilution: it is 1 in an ideal solution. A value above 1 raises the chemical potential relative to that ideal solution; below 1 lowers it. It is not a mole fraction and need not lie between 0 and 1.

Vapor-liquid equilibrium requires equal fugacities, so activity coefficients alone are not a complete vapor-liquid calculation: pure-component reference fugacities and a vapor model are also needed. A liquid-liquid calculation with the same standard states can cancel those reference terms, but still requires stability and phase-split calculations.

Choosing a model

Available information / goal Starting point
Reliable fitted binary liquid-mixture parameters NRTL or UNIQUAC
Molecular structures, but no fitted molecular pair parameters UNIFAC family, provided all required groups and interactions are covered
An excess model intended for Huron-Vidal cubic-EOS coupling NRTL-HV, with matching EOS covolumes and fitted parameters

Parameterized models can represent the data used to fit them well, but the model name alone supplies no parameters. Group-contribution models reuse interactions between chemical fragments across molecules; they are predictive estimates, not a guarantee of accuracy for a new system. Choose using measured data near your temperature/composition and the intended equilibrium type, not just whether a constructor accepts the inputs.

Inputs, units, and shared methods

  • n(:) contains component mole amounts, with n >= 0 and sum(n) > 0. Component order must match all model vectors and matrix indices. A mole-fraction vector is valid as a one-mole basis; mass fractions are not interchangeable.
  • T is absolute temperature in kelvin, never degrees Celsius.
  • ln_activity_coefficient returns natural logarithms; use exp to get . Optional derivatives are with respect to T or mole amounts, not independently varied mole fractions.
  • The gas constant yaeos__constants uses bar L/(mol K). Thus extensive Ge and He are in bar L, and Se and Cpe are in bar L/K when n is in mol. Multiply by 100 to convert bar L to joules. Divide by sum(n) for molar properties.
  • Scaling every amount by the same factor leaves activity coefficients unchanged and scales extensive properties by that factor. Mole-number derivatives depend on the chosen amount basis (the dlngammadn matrix scales inversely).

These implementations have no explicit pressure argument in their excess-property methods. They cannot supply pressure-dependent liquid volumes or replace a full EOS at high pressure. Each concrete model supplies excess_gibbs; the shared methods derive the properties below from it.

Because the shared methods are inherited from GeModel, the same calls work for every model on this site. That uniformity is a convenience, not a statement that the models are interchangeable: they differ in how they describe nonideality, in how many parameters they need, and in where they are reliable.

Worked example: reading a result

The complete program below evaluates an ethanol–water mixture with UNIFAC. It is worth running once and reading the output carefully, because the numbers are easily misread.

program excess_properties
use yaeos, only: pr, Groups, setup_unifac, UNIFAC
implicit none

type(UNIFAC) :: model

integer, parameter :: nc = 2

type(Groups) :: molecules(nc)

real(pr) :: n(nc)
real(pr) :: T
real(pr) :: ln_gamma(nc)
real(pr) :: dln_gamma_dT(nc)
real(pr) :: dln_gamma_dn(nc, nc)
real(pr) :: Ge
real(pr) :: He
real(pr) :: Se
real(pr) :: Cpe

T = 298.15_pr
n = [0.5_pr, 0.5_pr]

! Ethanol, then water: output arrays follow this same order.
molecules(1)%groups_ids = [1, 2, 14]
molecules(1)%number_of_groups = [1, 1, 1]
molecules(2)%groups_ids = [16]
molecules(2)%number_of_groups = [1]

! setup UNIFAC model
model = setup_unifac(molecules)

! Calculate ln_gamma and its derivatives
call model%ln_activity_coefficient(n, T, ln_gamma, dln_gamma_dT, dln_gamma_dn)

print *, "ln_gamma = ", ln_gamma
print *, "dln_gamma_dT = ", dln_gamma_dT
print *, "dln_gamma_dn = ", dln_gamma_dn
print *, "gamma = ", exp(ln_gamma)
call model%excess_gibbs(n, T, Ge=Ge)
call model%excess_enthalpy(n, T, He=He)
call model%excess_entropy(n, T, Se=Se)
call model%excess_Cp(n, T, Cpe)
print *, "GE, HE [bar L] = ", Ge, He
print *, "SE, CpE [bar L/K] = ", Se, Cpe
print *, "GE - (HE - T*SE), near zero = ", Ge - (He - T*Se)
end program excess_properties

This is a complete program. The first output entry refers to ethanol and the second to water. Negative ln_gamma is allowed; exp(ln_gamma) must be positive. The final energy identity is a useful consistency check, but does not validate the model against experiments. A positive He means mixing absorbs heat relative to ideal-solution enthalpy at that temperature; Cpe is only the excess, not the total heat capacity.

To interpret the output, work through it in this order.

  1. ln_gamma is a logarithm. exp(ln_gamma) gives the coefficient. A coefficient above 1 means the component is less stable in the mixture than ideal dilution would predict; below 1 means more stable. Neither the logarithm nor the coefficient is a mole fraction, and neither is bounded by 1.
  2. Multiply by the mole fraction for an activity. x_i * gamma_i is the dimensionless activity, the quantity that appears in the chemical potential. A large gamma_i for a component with a small x_i may still mean a small activity.
  3. The energy outputs are extensive. Ge and He are in bar·L for the whole n vector (1 mol here, so numerically molar); Se and Cpe are in bar·L/K. Multiply by 100 for J and J/K. Divide by sum(n) for molar values. A positive Ge means mixing is less favorable than ideal mixing; a positive He means mixing absorbs heat.
  4. dln_gamma_dn is a mole-number derivative, so it scales inversely with the amount basis: multiplying n by ten divides these entries by ten, while ln_gamma itself is unchanged. dln_gamma_dT is unaffected by scaling.
  5. The identity check. Ge - (He - T*Se) must vanish at the level of round-off, because . A nonzero value means a bug in the model or in your call, not a physical finding.

What this example does not tell you is whether the prediction is accurate. That requires comparing with measured data for the same mixture.

Checks, limits, and next steps

Before using a phase-equilibrium result, work through the inputs once more. Confirm the component order matches the model, that the temperature is inside the range the parameters were fitted for, that the signs and units of the parameters are what you think they are, and, for the group-contribution models, that every interaction your mixture needs is actually covered. Scale n and confirm the activity coefficients do not change. In the pure-component limit the coefficient of the present component should approach 1 for a consistently referenced model; a trace solute need not have a coefficient of 1. Never evaluate an all-zero amount vector.

A large activity coefficient does not by itself prove that a liquid splits. Whether a feed is unstable is a separate question, answered by a stability analysis and a phase-equilibrium calculation; see the phase-equilibrium guide. And a converged prediction is not an accurate one: compare with measured data before drawing conclusions from it.

For EOS coupling, continue to the cubic mixing rules. Standard NRTL, UNIQUAC, and UNIFAC are not general electrolyte, reacting-mixture, or near-critical models, and they supply no volumetric properties on their own.
