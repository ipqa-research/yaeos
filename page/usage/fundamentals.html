Thermodynamics essentials for using yaeos

You do not need to derive an equation of state to use yaeos. You do need to know what you are specifying, what the model returns, and what checks make a result believable. This page introduces those ideas before the more detailed model and calculation guides.

If you have not run a calculation yet, start with the getting-started example. You can return here whenever a guide uses an unfamiliar term.

A component is not a phase

A component is one chemical species in the model, such as methane or ethane. A phase is a physically distinct region, such as a liquid or a vapor. A liquid mixture can contain several components, and a pure component can coexist as both liquid and vapor. Two different liquids can also coexist.

For example, a methane–ethane feed has two components whether it is entirely vapor or has separated into a liquid and a vapor. In the separated state, both phases generally contain both components, but in different proportions.

This distinction matters when sizing arrays: a two-component composition has two entries, not one entry per phase.

A feed must have a positive total amount and nonnegative entries; logarithmic formulations may additionally require strictly positive entries.

Component order must never change silently

If the first constructor entry describes methane and the second ethane, every composition, parameter vector, and row/column of an interaction matrix must use that same order. Array sizes alone cannot catch a reversed composition or an incorrectly transposed interaction matrix.

One bar·litre is 100 J. For an extensive energy energy and an amount vector n, the molar energy in J/mol is 100.0_pr * energy / sum(n). Some equilibrium result fields are already molar quantities; check their documentation before dividing again. SAFT segment diameters and model-specific interaction parameters have their own units, described on their respective model pages.

What an equation of state does—and does not do

An equation of state (EOS) models how a fluid’s pressure, temperature, volume, and composition are related. The ideal-gas equation is the simplest:

Real molecules occupy space and interact. An EOS adds a description of those effects, making it possible to estimate liquid densities and non-ideal gas behavior. Different model families make different approximations; adding more parameters does not automatically make a model better for your mixture.

yaeos separates two jobs. A model evaluates properties for a proposed state. An equilibrium solver searches for the phases and compositions that can coexist. Calling pressure at an arbitrary volume evaluates the EOS; it does not ask whether the material would actually stay in a single phase.

Residual does not mean an error in a numerical calculation. It means a departure from an ideal-gas reference. A residual enthalpy is not the total enthalpy: the matching ideal-gas contribution is needed too. The reference held fixed—volume or pressure—also matters for some departure properties. The property reference explains those distinctions in detail.

An excess Gibbs energy, , uses a different reference: ideal mixing of components in specified pure-component reference states. It measures non-ideal mixing rather than departure from an ideal gas. Do not substitute an excess quantity for a residual one merely because both describe non-ideality.

Fugacity and activity: how models compare phases

Think of fugacity as a pressure-like measure of a component’s tendency to leave a phase. More precisely, it expresses chemical potential on a chosen reference scale. At equilibrium a component has the same chemical potential, and therefore the same fugacity, in all phases in which it is present.

A volume root is not a phase split

At a specified pressure and temperature, an EOS can have several mathematical volume solutions. A small-volume root is liquid-like; a large-volume root is vapor-like. Depending on the state there may be only one physically admissible root. The requested names "liquid" and "vapor" select root branches; they do not guarantee that two different phases exist.

The "stable" root requests the model’s preferred homogeneous root. A mixture at that composition may still lower its Gibbs energy by separating into phases with different compositions. That is the question answered by stability analysis and flash calculations, not by a volume call alone.

A checklist before using a prediction

  1. Check units, component ordering, and parameter provenance.
  2. Check whether the routine expects amounts or normalized compositions.
  3. Check its termination/status information before reading result arrays.
  4. Check compositions, phase fractions, and material balances.
  5. Check that a root or branch corresponds to the problem you intended to solve.
  6. Compare with limiting behavior or independent experimental data.

A converged solver has solved its equations to a numerical tolerance. It has not established that the parameters are appropriate, that a global search found every possible phase, or that the model is accurate outside its fitted range.
